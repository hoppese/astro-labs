What this shows

A planet doesn’t orbit a fixed star — both bodies circle their common centre of mass. The star traces a small circle, so half of each orbit it moves slightly toward us and half slightly away. That back-and-forth Doppler-shifts its spectral lines blue then red, by a few metres per second. Measure the size of the wobble and you get the planet’s mass (times sin i); measure its period and you get the orbit. This was how the first planet around a Sun-like star was found.

How to use

Hit Play. The top view shows the star and planet circling the barycentre (marked ×); the arrow is the star’s velocity, and the strip below is one spectral line sliding blue/red. The lower graph is the radial-velocity curve. Make the planet heavier or closer and the wobble grows; tilt the orbit toward face-on and it shrinks toward zero.

Relevant relationships
the star and planet balance about the barycentre.
radial-velocity semi-amplitude — the size of the wobble we measure.
the Doppler shift of the lines: a few m/s in a line at ~500 nm.

Worked example — the wobble from Jupiter ×

Jupiter (mass 1/1047 of the Sun) orbits at 5.2 AU with a period of 11.9 years. How fast does the Sun wobble, seen edge-on?

About 12 m/s — a walking pace, from 780 million km away. Earth would move the Sun just 0.09 m/s, which is near the current detection limit.
