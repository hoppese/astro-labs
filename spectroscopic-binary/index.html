Spectroscopic Binary ×

What this shows

Most binaries are too tight to split apart in any telescope — they look like a single point. But split their light into a spectrum and you see two sets of spectral lines sliding back and forth past each other as the stars swing toward and away from us. The size of each star’s line shift is its orbital speed; the heavier star moves slower and stays closer to the centre of mass, so the speed ratio hands you the mass ratio directly.

How to use

Watch the top view (velocity arrows), the spectrum (one absorption line splitting into two), and the radial-velocity curve. Change the masses: the lighter star’s line swings wider. Change the period: a tighter orbit is faster, so both lines swing wider. When the stars move across our line of sight (phase 0.25, 0.75) the lines merge — no shift.

Relevant relationships
the mass ratio straight from the two velocity amplitudes.
Doppler shift of the lines from the line-of-sight speed.
Kepler’s third law gives the total mass once the orbit size and period are known.

Worked example — mass ratio from line shifts ×

In a double-lined spectroscopic binary, star A’s lines swing by ±40 km/s and star B’s by ±120 km/s over a 4-day period. What is the mass ratio?

1 · Speed ratio → mass ratio
2 · Which is heavier?
Star A moves slower, so it is the heavier one: M_A = 3 M_B. The heavy star barely budges while the light one whips around it.
3 · Total mass
Combine the summed speed (160 km/s) and the 4-day period with Kepler’s third law to get M_A + M_B, then split it 3:1. (An edge-on orbit is assumed; otherwise you only get a lower limit.)
