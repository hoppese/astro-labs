Spectroscopic Binary

What this shows

Many binaries are too tight to split apart in any telescope: they look like a single point. But split their light into a spectrum and you see two sets of spectral lines sliding back and forth past each other as the stars swing toward and away from us. The size of each star’s line shift is its orbital speed; the heavier star moves slower and stays closer to the centre of mass, so the speed ratio hands you the mass ratio directly.

How to use

Watch the 3D orbit view (velocity arrows, dashed arrow toward Earth), the spectrum (one absorption line splitting into two) and the radial-velocity curve. Change the masses: the lighter star’s line swings wider. Shorten the period: a tighter orbit is faster, so both lines swing wider. When the stars move across our line of sight (phase 0.25 and 0.75) the lines merge: no shift. Lower the inclination to tilt the orbit toward face-on; both swings shrink by sin i, but their ratio does not change.

Relevant relationships
the mass ratio straight from the two velocity amplitudes.
Doppler shift of the lines from the line-of-sight speed.

Worked example — mass ratio from line shifts

In a double-lined spectroscopic binary, star A’s lines swing by ±40 km/s and star B’s by ±120 km/s over a 4-day period. What is the mass ratio?

1 · Speed ratio → mass ratio
2 · Which is heavier?
Star A moves slower, so it is the heavier one: . The heavy star stays close to the centre of mass while the light one swings around it.
3 · Orbit size from the speeds
The two stars together cover a circle of radius a = aA + aB at a combined speed of 160 km/s.
4 · Total mass from Kepler’s third law
Split 3 : 1, that is 1.27 M⊙ for A and 0.42 M⊙ for B. This assumes an edge-on orbit; if it is tilted, these are lower limits. Set A = 1.3, B = 0.4, P = 4 days to check.
