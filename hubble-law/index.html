Hubble’s Law

What this shows

Almost every galaxy’s light is redshifted, and the more distant ones are redshifted more, in proportion to their distance. That’s not because we’re at the centre of an explosion; it’s because space itself is expanding, so every galaxy sees every other one receding, faster the farther away it is. Run it backwards and everything meets at one moment: the Big Bang, roughly 1/H₀ ago.

How to use

Hit Run the expansion: watch the galaxies drift apart, each with a velocity arrow proportional to its distance. The graph plots speed against distance: a straight line whose slope is H₀. Click a different galaxy to re-centre on it: same straight line, same slope. Change H₀ and the line gets steeper or shallower, and the implied age of the universe changes with it. Tick realistic data to add each galaxy’s own motion and a distance error, as in real measurements; the green best-fit line still recovers H₀.

Worked example — distance to a galaxy from its redshift

A galaxy’s spectral lines are shifted so that Δλ/λ = 0.02. Taking H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc, how far away is it, and what does H₀ imply for the age of the universe?

1 · Redshift → speed
2 · Speed → distance
3 · Age ≈ 1/H₀
1/H₀ = (3.086×10¹⁹ km) / (70 km/s) = 4.4×10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years, close to the 13.8 Gyr found from more careful models.
