Atomic Spectra

What this shows

An electron in an atom can only have certain energies, its energy levels. When it drops to a lower level the atom emits one photon carrying exactly the energy difference; to jump up it must absorb a photon with exactly that energy. So each element emits and absorbs only its own set of wavelengths: a fingerprint. Hydrogen’s drops to n = 2 (the Balmer series) give the visible red, blue-green, blue and violet lines; drops to n = 1 (Lyman) are ultraviolet; drops to n = 3 (Paschen) are infrared.

Kirchhoff’s laws. (1) A hot, dense object glows with a continuous spectrum. (2) A hot, thin gas seen against a dark background gives bright emission lines. (3) A cooler, thin gas in front of a hotter continuous source gives dark absorption lines at the same wavelengths. Star spectra are type 3.

How to use

Hydrogen atom: click a level (or a number chip) to move the electron; the photon it gives off or needs appears with its wavelength, and the line is recorded on the strip (top row emitted, bottom row absorbed). Fire single photons with the slider: only the right wavelengths are absorbed, and a photon with more than enough energy ionizes the atom.

Kirchhoff’s laws: three spectroscopes look at the same set-up; change the gas and the two temperatures. Make the gas hotter than the source to flip view 2 to bright lines. Identify a gas: match the mystery spectrum to the reference fingerprints.

Worked example: the blue-green line of hydrogen

An electron in a hydrogen atom drops from n = 4 to n = 2. What is the energy and the wavelength of the photon, and can you see it? Where would a cool hydrogen cloud in front of a hot star show this line?

1 · The two energy levels
2 · The photon carries the difference
3 · Convert energy to wavelength
486 nm is blue-green visible light: the Hβ line of the Balmer series. (With more digits, 486.1 nm in air.)
4 · Absorption
An electron sitting in n = 2 can jump to n = 4 only by absorbing a photon of exactly 2.55 eV, so cool hydrogen in front of a hot star removes light at the same 486 nm: a dark line where the emission spectrum has a bright one. Try it: put the electron on n = 2, set the photon to 486.1 nm, and fire.
