Inverse-Square Brightness

What this shows

A star pours out a fixed amount of light per second: its luminosity. By the time that light reaches you it has spread over a sphere, and a sphere twice as big has four times the area, so the light you actually catch (the flux, in watts per square metre) drops as 1/distance². That is why a nearby dim star can outshine a distant brilliant one, and why you can’t tell a star’s true power from how bright it looks without also knowing how far away it is.

How to use

Pick a star or set a luminosity and a distance (both sliders are logarithmic). The 3D picture shows the same light passing through 1, 4 and 9 squares at distances d, 2d and 3d; the panel beside it is how the star looks from your chosen distance, with its place on the magnitude scale. The graph is flux against distance on log–log axes, where 1/d² is a straight line of slope −2; where it crosses the blue dashed line the star drops below naked-eye visibility. Apparent magnitude is how bright the star looks; absolute magnitude is how bright it would look from a standard 10 pc.

Relevant relationships
flux = luminosity spread over a sphere of radius d.
the magnitude scale: 5 magnitudes = a factor of 100 in flux.
the distance modulus: the gap between how bright a star looks and how bright it really is.

Worked example — Sirius vs the Sun

Sirius is 25 times as luminous as the Sun and lies 2.6 pc away. The Sun is only 1 AU away. How does the Sun’s flux at Earth compare with the flux we get from Sirius?

1 · Put both distances in the same unit
1 AU = 4.85×10⁻⁶ pc, so Sirius (2.6 pc) is about 540,000× farther away than the Sun.
2 · Flux ratio
3 · Interpret
The Sun looks about 10 billion times brighter than Sirius, even though Sirius is intrinsically 25× more powerful. Distance wins, overwhelmingly.
