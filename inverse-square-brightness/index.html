Inverse-Square Brightness ×

What this shows

A star pours out a fixed amount of light per second — its luminosity. By the time that light reaches you it has spread over a sphere, and a sphere twice as big has four times the area, so the light you actually catch — the flux — drops as 1/distance². That is why a nearby dim star can outshine a distant brilliant one, and why you can’t tell a star’s true power from how bright it looks without also knowing how far away it is.

How to use

Set a luminosity and a distance. The scene shows the same light spreading thinner as it travels out; the swatch and the bar are how bright the star looks from here. The graph is flux vs distance — a steep 1/d² fall-off. Apparent magnitude is what you see; absolute magnitude is what it would look like from a standard 10 pc.

Relevant relationships
flux = luminosity spread over a sphere of radius d.
the magnitude scale: 5 magnitudes = a factor of 100 in flux.
the distance modulus — the gap between how bright a star looks and how bright it really is.

Worked example — Sirius vs the Sun ×

Sirius is 25 times as luminous as the Sun and lies 2.6 pc away. The Sun is only 1 AU away. How does the Sun’s flux at Earth compare with the flux we get from Sirius?

1 AU = 4.85×10⁻⁶ pc; Sirius is 2.6 pc, about 540,000× farther.

3 · Interpret
The Sun looks about 10 billion times brighter than Sirius, even though Sirius is intrinsically 25× more powerful. Distance wins, overwhelmingly.
