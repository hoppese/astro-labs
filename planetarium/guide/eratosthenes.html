How big is the Earth? Eratosthenes and the noon Sun

Eratosthenes, about 240 BC

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

On the same day, at noon, the Sun stands at different heights in the sky in two cities. Because the Sun is so far away that its light arrives in parallel rays, the difference is the angle between the two cities at the centre of the Earth. Add how far apart the cities are, and you have the size of the whole Earth.

What you will find out

The circumference of the Earth, in the stadia Eratosthenes used, from two noon measurements of the Sun and the distance between two cities. It is the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder: the eclipse and moonrise labs measure the Moon in Earth sizes, and this lab turns Earth sizes into a real length.

You need: the planetarium (the lab puts you at Syene, modern Aswan, on the summer solstice of 241 BC, with Alexandria as a second observer), and a calculator.

The idea: parallel sunlight

The Sun is very far away compared with the size of the Earth, so its rays reach every part of the Earth travelling in the same direction: they are parallel. But the Earth is round, so “straight up” points in a different direction in every city. The farther apart two cities are (north to south), the more their “straight up” directions differ.

So if you know θ and the distance along the ground between the two cities, the whole Earth follows by proportion: the arc between the cities is the same fraction of the whole circumference as θ is of a full circle.

θ360° = distance between the citiescircumference of the Earth
socircumference = 360°θ × distance between the cities

Eratosthenes was told that the two cities were 5000 stadia apart (a stadion was a Greek unit of length of about 160–190 m; which one he used is still argued about).

Measuring the angle: the Sun at noon

Eratosthenes measured the angle of a stick’s shadow. In the planetarium you read the Sun’s altitude, its angle above the horizon, which comes to the same thing. The moment to read it is local noon, when the Sun crosses the meridian, the north–south line through the point straight overhead: it is then at its highest for the day.

The Sun need not be exactly overhead at Syene. What matters is the difference between the two cities:

θ = (distance from the zenith at Alexandria) − (distance from the zenith at Syene)
soθ = (altitude at Syene) − (altitude at Alexandria)

This works because Alexandria lies (almost) due north of Syene, and both measurements are made at each city’s own noon, on the same day.

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. Noon at Syene. The lab opens at Syene on the morning of the solstice, the Sun selected and followed, with the meridian drawn as a green line. Step the clock forward (Shift + ↑ for a minute, Shift + → for a second) until the Sun’s centre sits on the green line. (The year shows as −0240: astronomers count a year 0, so 241 BC is −240.)
  2. Read the altitude. In the Sun’s panel, the second number of Az / Alt is its altitude. Write it down to the arcsecond. Check that you have the highest point: a minute either side, the altitude is a little lower.
  3. Noon at Alexandria. Press Swap observers (in the step, or in the Two observers panel) to stand in Alexandria on the same day. Alexandria is a little west of Syene, so its noon comes a few minutes later: step on until the Sun is on the green line again, and read the altitude.
  4. Work it out. Subtract to get θ, then use the proportion above with 5000 stadia.

What can go wrong

  • Not quite noon. Near noon the Sun’s altitude changes very slowly, so a minute early or late costs only a little, but it does cost something. Find the highest altitude, or the moment the Sun is on the green line, in each city.
  • Mixing degrees, minutes and seconds. Turn both altitudes into decimal degrees (1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″) before subtracting, or subtract each part carefully.
  • The cities are not exactly north–south. Alexandria is about 3° of longitude west of Syene, so the distance between them is not all north–south. Eratosthenes ignored this; so does this lab.
  • Syene is not exactly on the tropic. The Sun was not quite overhead there. That is why you take the difference of two altitudes rather than assume 90° at Syene.
  • Refraction. The air lifts the Sun a little. The lab switches refraction off; at these high altitudes it would change each reading by only a fraction of an arcminute, and the difference even less.
  • The stadion. Your answer comes out in stadia. Turning it into kilometres depends on which stadion Eratosthenes meant, which is the largest uncertainty of all.

Going further

  • With the Earth’s size known, the eclipse lab gives the Moon’s size, and the moonrise lab its distance.
  • Instead of the distance between Syene and Alexandria, use their latitudes (in Settings → Location). How does the difference in latitude compare with your θ? Why should it?
  • Try it from home: pick two places due north and south of each other, on any date, and measure the Sun at each one’s noon.
