The Earth’s shadow on the Moon

Aristotle, about 350 BC; Aristarchus, about 270 BC

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

During a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through the Earth’s shadow. Its edge tells you the Earth is round, and timing the Moon across it tells you how big the Moon is compared with the Earth: the first two rungs of the cosmic distance ladder, both from about 2,300 years ago.

What you will find out

Two things, from one eclipse (27 July 2018, the longest of the century, seen from Nairobi):

  • The shape of the Earth. The edge of the shadow on the Moon is always part of a circle. Aristotle (about 350 BC) argued that only a ball casts a round shadow from every direction.
  • The size of the Moon, in Earth sizes. How wide is the Earth’s shadow at the Moon, measured in Moon diameters? Aristarchus (about 270 BC) turned that into the Moon’s size compared with the Earth’s.

You need: the planetarium (the lab sets the date, the place and the view), the stopwatch it opens, a calculator, and somewhere to write down four times.

Shadows from a big light: umbra and penumbra

A small lamp casts a sharp shadow. The Sun is not small: it is a disc half a degree across, so light reaches the space behind the Earth from many directions at once. That splits the Earth’s shadow into two parts.

  • The umbra is where the Earth hides the whole Sun. No sunlight arrives at all. It is a long cone that narrows behind the Earth, because the Sun is bigger than the Earth.
  • The penumbra surrounds it. There the Earth hides only part of the Sun, so the light is dimmed but not gone. It widens with distance.
What you will see in the planetariumThe penumbra is faint: the Moon in it is only a little dimmer, and many people never notice it. The umbra is obvious: a dark, often coppery bite out of the Moon. This lab times the umbra.

The shape of the shadow

Between U1 and U2 the umbra covers more and more of the Moon, and the boundary between the dark part and the bright part is the edge of the Earth’s shadow. Watch it as the clock runs:

  • Is the edge ever a straight line? Is it ever curved the other way (bulging into the dark part)?
  • How big would the whole circle be that this arc belongs to? Compare it with the Moon by eye.

Aristotle’s argument: eclipses happen with the Sun, Earth and Moon in many different arrangements, at every time of night and year, and the shadow’s edge is always an arc of a circle. A disc (a flat round Earth) would cast an oval or a thin line when seen edge-on. Only a sphere casts a round shadow from every direction.

The size: timing the Moon across the shadow

The Moon moves through the shadow at a steady speed, so time is proportional to distance. Follow the Moon’s leading edge in the figure above:

  • from U1 to U2 it moves by one Moon diameter (from touching the shadow to being just inside it);
  • from U1 to U3 it crosses the whole umbra.

Dividing one by the other, the speed cancels:

shadow diameter ÷ Moon diameter = (t3 − t1) ÷ (t2 − t1)

You can check it with the other pair: from U2 to U4 the trailing edge also crosses the whole umbra, and from U3 to U4 the Moon moves one diameter. Averaging the two estimates helps.

A chord, not a diameterThe Moon does not always pass through the middle of the shadow. Off-center, it crosses a chord of the circle, shorter than the diameter. In this eclipse it passes very close to the center, so the chord is nearly the full width. In other eclipses you would have to correct for it.

From the shadow to the Earth

The shadow at the Moon is not as wide as the Earth: the umbra narrows on its way out (the cone in the first figure). By how much? This is the clever part.

Earth diameter ≈ shadow diameter + 1 Moon diameter

So once you know the shadow’s width in Moon diameters, add one and you have the Earth’s width in Moon diameters. Turn it round for the Moon’s size as a fraction of the Earth’s.

In the planetarium, step by step

  1. Find the first contact (U1). The lab starts a little before it, zoomed in on the Moon. Run the clock (L faster, J slower, 7 stop) until the dark edge is about to touch the Moon, stop it, then step a second or a minute at a time (Shift + → is 1 second, Shift + ↑ 1 minute) to the first moment the edge touches. Press Mark time in the stopwatch (or X).
  2. Look at the shape. Step on a few minutes at a time through the partial phase and watch the edge of the shadow on the Moon (the section above says what to look for). Write down your estimate of how big the whole circle is, in Moon diameters.
  3. Totality begins (U2). Step to the moment the Moon is completely inside the umbra: the last bright sliver disappears. Mark it.
  4. Totality ends (U3). Run the clock on (this eclipse’s totality is long: well over an hour) and step to the moment the Moon’s leading edge first brightens. Mark it.
  5. Last contact (U4). Step to the moment the Moon is completely clear of the umbra. Mark it.
  6. Work it out. In the stopwatch, press two mark numbers to see the time between them. Fill in the table below and use the formulas above.

What can go wrong

  • Judging the instant. The edge of the umbra is not perfectly sharp, and a contact is a judgement. Zoom in, and step backward and forward over the moment. Being a minute off at U1 matters most, because U1 → U2 is the shorter interval.
  • The penumbra. Do not time the faint penumbra by mistake: the umbra is the dark, distinct edge.
  • The Earth’s air. Our atmosphere makes the real shadow about 1–2% bigger than a bare rock Earth would cast; eclipse predictions (and the planetarium) allow for it.
  • The chord. See the warning above: here it is a small effect.
