The Sun · lab guide

How big is the Sun? The same size as the Moon in the sky

Aristarchus

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

In a total eclipse the Moon only just covers the Sun. Two things that look the same size have their true sizes in the same ratio as their distances, so once you know how many times farther away the Sun is, you know how many times bigger it is.

What you will find out

How many times bigger the Sun really is than the Moon. This is the second half of Aristarchus’s argument, about 270 BC: the half Moon gave him how many times farther away the Sun is, and an eclipse shows that the two look the same size. Together they give the Sun’s true size in Moon sizes, and then, with the Moon’s size, in Earth sizes.

You need: the planetarium (the lab puts you in Richmond, Indiana, on 8 April 2024, during the partial phase of the total solar eclipse, with the Sun selected and followed), the angle tool, your result from the half-Moon lab, and a calculator.

The idea: the same angle

How big something looks depends on two things: how big it really is, and how far away. A coin at arm’s length can cover the full Moon. When two things look exactly the same size, the lines from your eye that graze the edges of the near one also graze the edges of the far one.

For a small angle, measured in radians, the angle is the diameter over the distance, for each body:

α = Moon’s diameterMoon’s distance = Sun’s diameterSun’s distance
soSun’s diameterMoon’s diameter = Sun’s distanceMoon’s distance
soSun’s diameter = Sun’s distanceMoon’s distance × Moon’s diameter

The fraction on the right is exactly what the half-Moon lab measures. You do not need either distance in kilometres, only their ratio.

If the two angular sizes are not quite equal (and today they are not), the rule still works with one more factor: each diameter is its angular size times its distance, so

Sun’s diameterMoon’s diameter = Sun’s angular sizeMoon’s angular size × Sun’s distanceMoon’s distance

Measuring two discs that overlap

During the partial phase the Moon covers part of the Sun. The lab switches the atmosphere off, so that the dark Moon can be seen outside the Sun too. (In a real sky the part of the Moon off the Sun has the color of the sky and cannot be seen.) A diameter is measured from one edge to the opposite edge, through the center, and both edges must be ones you can see.

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. Measure both diameters. Open the angle tool (R). Click near one edge of the Sun, then near the opposite edge, across the line joining the centers as in the figure, and check that the row reads Sun limb to Sun limb. Do the same for the Moon (Moon limb to Moon limb). Write both in arcminutes, to the hundredth. Zooming in (+ or the mouse wheel) makes the clicks more precise.
  2. The ratio of the true diameters. Use the rule above, with the ratio of the distances from your half-Moon result.
  3. Totality. Run the clock on (Shift + ↑ steps a minute) until the Sun is completely covered, and measure both again. Which of the two is slightly bigger today? Does that agree with the eclipse being total?
  4. An annular eclipse. Open the Events finder (Labs menu) and choose Solar eclipses (anywhere on Earth). Find one marked annular and go to it: it does not matter whether it is seen from Richmond, because you can measure both diameters even where the Moon misses the Sun. Which is bigger now? What can go wrong

  • A hidden edge. Clicking along the line of centers puts one end of the measurement where the two discs overlap, and the tool may snap to the wrong body’s edge. Read the row: both ends must name the same body.
  • Center instead of edge. A click well inside a disc snaps to its center, which measures a radius, not a diameter. Click close to the edge.
  • Not through the center. Two edge points that are not opposite each other measure a chord, shorter than the diameter. The tool puts each point on the limb where you clicked, so click as nearly opposite as you can, or measure a few times and keep the largest.
  • Different moments. Both angular sizes change slowly, and not by the same amount. Measure the two at the same instant.
  • The half-Moon ratio. The answer here can only be as good as the distance ratio you bring from the half-Moon lab, and that ratio is very sensitive to the angle you measured there. Carry its uncertainty through.

Going further

  • The Sun in Earth sizes. The lunar eclipse lab gives the Moon’s diameter in Earth diameters. Multiply: the Sun’s diameter in Earth diameters. Aristarchus did this and found the Sun far bigger than the Earth, which is said to be why he put the Sun, not the Earth, at the center.
  • In kilometres. With the Earth’s size (the noon Sun lab) or the Moon’s distance (the two-observer parallax lab), every size and distance in this chain can be put in kilometres.
