What you will find out

  • The synodic month: from one full Moon to the next (phase to phase).
  • The sidereal month: the time the Moon takes to come back beside the same stars (star to star).
  • The lunar day: the time from the Moon crossing your meridian to crossing it again the next day.
  • Why the first is longer than the second, and how all three fit one rule.

You need: the planetarium (the lab starts at a full Moon, seen from Richmond, with the ground and the air switched off so the Moon is always in view), the Events finder and the stopwatch it opens, and a calculator.

The idea: one runner lapping another

Seen from the Earth, the Moon moves eastward against the stars, all the way round the sky in one sidereal month. The Sun moves the same way, much more slowly: all the way round in one year.

The Moon’s phase depends on where it is compared with the Sun. Full Moon is the Moon opposite the Sun; new Moon is the Moon beside it. So the Moon is full again only when it has gained a whole lap on the Sun, like a fast runner lapping a slow one. Because the Sun has moved on in the meantime, the Moon must go round once and a bit more: the synodic month is longer than the sidereal month.

One rule for the two months

Count in laps per day. The Moon goes once round the sky in one sidereal month, so each day it covers 1sidereal month of a lap. The Sun covers 11 year of a lap a day. The Moon gains on the Sun by the difference, and it has gained one whole lap after one synodic month:

1sidereal month − 11 year = 1synodic month
sosidereal month = synodic month × 1 year1 year + synodic month

All three times in days (a year is 365.25 days). So the synodic month, which is easy to measure (just count full Moons), gives you the sidereal month, which you can then check against the stars.

The lunar day: the same rule again

The lunar day is another case of one runner lapping another. Your meridian, the north–south line overhead, is carried round once a day by the turning Earth (once a day compared with the Sun). The Moon moves the same way, but slowly: it gains a lap on the Sun only once a synodic month. Your meridian catches the Moon again after one full turn of the Earth and a bit more.

11 day − 1synodic month = 11 lunar day
so1 lunar day = 1 day × synodic monthsynodic month − 1 day

Here “1 day” is the ordinary 24-hour day, from the Sun on your meridian to the Sun on your meridian, and the synodic month is in days too. The answer comes out in days: multiply by 24 for hours.

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. The synodic month (phase to phase). The Events finder opens on the list of the Moon’s phases. Write down the dates of several full Moons in a row. Their dates alone are only good to a day, so don’t use a single month: take the time from the first full Moon to the last and divide by the number of months in between.
  2. The sidereal month (star to star). The Moon is selected and the view follows it. Note which stars it is beside now: sketch them, or better, write down the Ecliptic lon in the Moon’s panel (its position along the Sun’s path, measured against the stars). Then step forward a day at a time with the +1 day button in the date box until it is nearly back, and finish with +1 h until it is back beside the same stars (the same ecliptic longitude). How long did that take?
  3. The lunar day (meridian to meridian). Switch on the meridian (;, the green line from north to south) and zoom in on the Moon. Run the clock to the instant the line cuts the Moon in half: Shift + ↓ ↑ steps a minute, Shift + ← → a second. Mark it (X). Do the same the next day, and press the two mark numbers to get the time between them.
  4. Why the difference? Explain, with the first figure, why the synodic month is about two days longer than the sidereal month. Then use the two rules above: does your synodic month predict your sidereal month, and your lunar day?

What can go wrong

  • Dates without times. The Events list gives each full Moon’s date. One month from dates alone can be a day out; averaging over five or six months shrinks that.
  • One month is not the average month. The Moon’s orbit is an oval, and it moves faster when it is nearer (see the Moon’s oval orbit). A single sidereal month, or a single lunar day, can differ from the long-run average by hours. Your measurement is right for these dates.
  • Where you stand moves the Moon. The Moon is close enough that people in different places, or you at different times of day, see it in slightly different places among the stars (that shift is the lunar parallax). The ecliptic longitude in the panel is the one seen from Richmond, so compare readings taken at about the same time of day.
  • Judging “cut in half”. Zoom in far enough that the Moon is large on the screen, and step back and forth over the instant.

Going further

  • The moonrise lab uses the lunar day to turn a rise time into the Moon’s size in the sky. Use the lunar day you measured here.
  • The Babylonians found that 19 years are almost exactly 235 synodic months (the Metonic cycle, still used to set the date of Easter). How close is it with your synodic month?
  • The rule works for planets too: a planet’s synodic period (from one opposition to the next) and its true period round the Sun are linked the same way. See periods of the planets.
