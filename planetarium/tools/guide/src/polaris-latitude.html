What you will find out

Your latitude, how far north of the equator you are, from nothing but the height of a star, and how far the pole star, Polaris, is from the true pole. Navigators found their latitude this way for centuries.

It is the other half of the Eratosthenes lab: latitude tells you where on the round Earth you are, and two latitudes plus the distance between them give the size of the Earth.

You need: the planetarium (the lab puts you in Richmond, Indiana, looking north at Polaris, with refraction switched off), the stopwatch it opens, and a calculator.

The idea: the pole stands at your latitude

As the Earth turns, the sky seems to turn about a fixed point straight out along the Earth’s axis: the celestial pole. That point is so far away that the direction to it is the same from everywhere on the Earth: parallel to the axis. But your horizon tilts as you go round the curve of the Earth. At the North Pole the celestial pole is straight overhead; on the equator it sits on the horizon; in between, it stands as high above your northern horizon as you are north of the equator.

latitude = altitude of the celestial pole

So if there were a star exactly at the pole, its altitude would be your latitude, at any hour of the night.

The pole star is not quite at the pole

Polaris is close to the celestial pole, but not on it. Like every other star it goes round the pole once a day, on a small circle. It is at its highest when it crosses the meridian above the pole, and at its lowest twelve hours later, crossing the meridian below the pole. The pole itself is exactly halfway between.

altitude of the pole = greatest altitude + least altitude2
distance of Polaris from the pole = greatest altitude − least altitude2

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. Read Polaris’s altitude. Polaris is selected and followed. In its panel, the second number of Az / Alt is its altitude. Write it down with the time.
  2. Step through a whole day. Press > to step the clock forward one hour (< steps back), reading the altitude each time, for 24 hours. Turn the readings into decimal degrees as you go (1° = 60′, 1′ = 60″). The meridian is drawn as the green line: Polaris is highest and lowest as it crosses it.
  3. Find the greatest and least. Near the top and bottom the altitude changes very slowly, so hourly steps get close. To be sure, go back to the hour with the greatest reading and step by minutes (Shift + ↑ or ↓) either side; do the same at the least.
  4. Work it out with the two equations above: the pole’s altitude (your latitude) and Polaris’s distance from the pole.
  5. Switch refraction on (F) and read the greatest and least altitudes again. How much does the air change your latitude?

What can go wrong

  • Taking Polaris for the pole. A single reading of Polaris’s altitude can be off by as much as the radius of its circle, at most hours of the night. That is the whole point of averaging the highest and the lowest.
  • Missing the turning point. Hour-by-hour readings can straddle the true highest and lowest moments. The altitude barely changes there, so the error is small, but check with minute steps.
  • Refraction. The air bends starlight and lifts every star a little, more the lower it is. It lifts both of Polaris’s readings by almost the same amount, so it does not cancel in the average: it moves your latitude by that amount. Real navigators corrected for it from tables.
  • Mixing degrees, minutes and seconds. Convert to decimal degrees before averaging.
  • The southern hemisphere. There is no bright star near the south celestial pole, so this exact method only works in the north. The idea still works with any star: average its highest and lowest altitudes, if it never sets.
