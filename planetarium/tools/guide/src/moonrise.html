What you will find out

The Moon’s angular diameter: how wide it looks, as an angle. With the Moon’s true size (the eclipse lab gives it in Earth sizes), that angle gives its distance, which is the step Aristarchus took after measuring the eclipse.

You need: the planetarium (the lab puts you on the equator at Quito, looking east, just before the full Moon rises), the stopwatch it opens, and a calculator.

The idea: the sky is a clock

As the Earth turns, the whole sky seems to turn the other way around us, steadily, about once a day. Everything in it, the Moon included, moves at the same steady rate, so the fraction of a whole turn (360°) that the Moon moves through is the same as the fraction of a whole turn’s time that passes.

For the Moon, “one whole turn” means the time it takes to come all the way round your sky and back to the same place, for instance from crossing your meridian (the north–south line overhead) one day to crossing it the next. That time is called one lunar day, and it is a little longer than 24 hours (see below). So:

angle360° = time taken1 lunar day
soangle = 360° × time taken1 lunar day

If you time how long the Moon takes to move by its own diameter, the same rule turns that time into the Moon’s angular diameter. A rising Moon is ideal for this, because the horizon is a sharp, straight ruler to time it against.

Why on the equator

On the equator, stars and the Moon (when it is near the celestial equator, as here) rise straight up. Farther north or south they rise at a slant, and the slant makes the timing longer: the Moon has to travel farther along its tilted path before it clears the horizon.

One whole turn: the lunar day

“Once a day” is not quite right for the Moon. While the Earth turns, the Moon moves along its own orbit in the same direction, about 13° a day. So after the Earth has turned once, the Moon is not back in the same place in your sky yet: the Earth has to turn a little more.

That is why the bottom of the fraction is 1 lunar day, measured from the Moon on your meridian to the Moon on your meridian the next day, and not 24 hours.

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. Mark 1: the top edge on the horizon. The lab starts a minute or two before the Moon rises, looking due east with refraction switched off. Step the clock a second at a time (Shift + →) to the instant the Moon’s top edge first reaches the horizon, and press Mark time (or X).
  2. Mark 2: the bottom edge clears. Step on to the instant the whole Moon is above the horizon, its bottom edge just touching it, and mark again. Press the two mark numbers to see the rise time in seconds.
  3. The lunar day. Switch on the meridian line (;). Select the Moon and press T to follow it. Run the clock (L to speed up, 7 to stop) to the moment the meridian cuts the Moon in half, step to the second, and mark. Do the same the next day. The time between the two marks is the lunar day.
  4. Work it out, with the equation above: both times in the same unit (seconds is easiest), and the answer in degrees. Convert to arcminutes (1° = 60′).
  5. Check by measuring. Go back to mark 2 (its ↩ button in the stopwatch), open the angle tool (R) and measure the Moon from edge to edge. How close is your timing?

What can go wrong

  • Refraction. The air bends light near the horizon, lifting the Moon and squashing it, so that it seems to rise later and slower. The lab switches refraction off; with it on (as in the real sky) the timing comes out too long. Try it.
  • Judging the instant. A second or two at each mark is a few percent of the rise time. Step back and forth over each moment.
  • The average lunar day. “24 h 50 min” is only an average; the real one varies by up to a quarter of an hour. Measure it for these dates.
  • Measuring at the wrong moment. The Moon’s angular size changes as its distance does, and from one place on the Earth it is also a little bigger overhead than on the horizon (you are then nearer to it by the Earth’s radius). Measure with the angle tool at mark 2, the moment you timed.
