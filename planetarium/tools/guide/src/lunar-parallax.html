What you will find out

The distance to the Moon, in kilometres and in Earth radii, by surveying: a baseline on the ground and an angle at the far end. In 1751 Lalande in Berlin and Lacaille at the Cape of Good Hope did this together, timing their observations to match, from almost opposite ends of the Old World.

You need: the planetarium (the lab puts you in Berlin, with a second observer in Cape Town at the same instant), the angle tool, the Earth seen square-on picture it opens, and a calculator.

The idea: parallax

The stars are so far away that both observers see them in the same places. The Moon is near, so each observer looks at it along a different line, and the two lines land on different stars. The Moon seems to sit in a different place among the stars for each of them. That shift is the parallax.

The farther away the Moon, the smaller the shift; the longer the baseline between the observers, the bigger. That is all the method needs.

The baseline: only the square-on part counts

The straight line between Berlin and Cape Town (through the Earth) is not quite square-on to the Moon: it is slanted to the line of sight. Imagine sliding one city along its line of sight toward the Moon: its view would not change at all. So only the part of the baseline that runs across the line of sight makes the shift.

From the angle to the distance

The baseline and the Moon make a long, thin triangle with the small angle p at the Moon. For a small angle, measured in radians, the angle is the short side over the long side:

p = bd
sod = bp
To put p in radians: p in radiansπ = p in degrees180°

To give the distance in Earth radii, divide it by the Earth’s radius, 6,371 km:

d in Earth radii = d in km6,371 km

In the planetarium, step by step

  2. Measure the shift. Open the angle tool (R). Click on the center of the solid Moon, then on the center of the dashed Moon: the tool snaps to centers. Read the angle and write it down, in degrees.

What can go wrong

  • Using the straight-line distance. The distance between the cities on a map, or straight through the Earth, is longer than the square-on part, and gives too large a distance to the Moon. Use the square-on picture.
  • Degrees in the formula. d = b over p only works with p in radians. In degrees the answer comes out about 57 times too small.
  • Placing the ruler. The ruler does not snap to the cities: drag from the center of one marker to the center of the other. Use Zoom to the places if the markers are small.
  • Center to center. Measure from the center of one Moon to the center of the other, not edge to edge. If you click near the edge of a disc, the tool snaps to the edge instead.
  • Which distance? Your answer is the Moon’s distance from the observers on the surface, which differs a little from its distance from the Earth’s center. It also changes from night to night, because the Moon’s orbit is an oval (the Moon’s oval orbit lab).
