What you will find out

Two things about the Moon’s orbit: the ratio of its greatest to its least distance, and its eccentricity, the number that says how far the orbit is from a circle. Hipparchus and Ptolemy knew the Moon’s distance changed; you will measure by how much.

You need: the planetarium (the lab opens at a perigee, looking at the Moon through a narrow view with the ground switched off), the Events finder it opens, the angle tool, and a calculator.

The idea: an oval, with the Earth off-center

The Moon’s orbit is an ellipse, an oval, and the Earth is not at its middle but at a point off to one side called a focus. So once each orbit the Moon passes its nearest point, perigee, and half an orbit later its farthest, apogee.

Nearer looks bigger

The Moon itself does not change size, but the nearer it is, the wider the angle it covers from your eye. For small angles the rule is simple: twice as far looks half as big. The angular size is inversely proportional to the distance.

So the ratio of the two sizes is the ratio of the two distances, the other way up:

size at perigeesize at apogee = distance at apogeedistance at perigee
sodistance at apogee = size at perigeesize at apogee × distance at perigee

You do not need to know either distance: the ratio comes out on its own.

From the two sizes to the eccentricity

Call the bigger angle (at perigee) big and the smaller one (at apogee) small. From the figure, the two distances are a(1 − e) and a(1 + e), and the sizes go the other way round, so:

bigsmall = a(1 + e)a(1 − e) = 1 + e1 − e
sobig (1 − e) = small (1 + e), and e = big − smallbig + small

The a cancels: the eccentricity, too, comes straight from the two angles.

In the planetarium, step by step

Open this lab in the planetarium ↗

  1. Find the two dates. The Events finder lists the Moon’s perigees and apogees. Click a perigee and the next apogee after it: the clock jumps to each. (If it says “below your horizon”, that does not matter: the ground is switched off, so you can see the Moon anyway.)
  2. Measure at perigee. With the angle tool (R), click one edge of the Moon and then the opposite edge, straight across its middle. Zoom in first so that each edge is sharp; work in arcminutes (1° = 60′).
  3. Measure at apogee. Click the apogee in the Events finder. The angle tool’s row follows the Moon, so you do not have to measure again: read the new value in the same row. Better still, measure afresh as well and compare.
  4. Work it out. The ratio of the two distances, then the eccentricity, with the equations above.
  5. Extra. On one night, measure the Moon when it has just risen and again when it is highest. Why is it a little different? (See below.)

What can go wrong

  • Where you measure from. The planetarium shows the Moon as seen from your place on the Earth’s surface, not from the Earth’s center. When the Moon is overhead you are one Earth radius nearer to it, so it looks slightly bigger; on the horizon, or below it, you are about as far away as the center is, or farther. That changes your two sizes a little, and so your eccentricity. It is the reason for the extra step.
  • Judging the edges. A small slip at an edge is a few percent of the difference between the two sizes, and the eccentricity depends on that difference. Zoom in and measure each one more than once.
  • Not every perigee is the same. The Sun tugs on the Moon’s orbit, so the orbit’s shape changes from month to month: some perigees are nearer than others. Your answer is for this pair; another pair gives a slightly different value.
  • The “supermoon”. A full Moon near perigee is sometimes called a supermoon. Your numbers show how much bigger it really is. Is that something you would notice by eye, with nothing beside it to compare?
