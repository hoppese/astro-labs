By the end of class, students can

  • explain the phases from one fact: the half of the Moon facing the Sun is lit, and we see a different share of that half as the Moon goes round us;
  • draw the geocentric picture (Earth with a person, the Sun, the Moon) for any phase and time of day, and read the phase or the time off such a picture;
  • say when a given phase rises, is highest, and sets (the full Moon rises at sunset, the first quarter is highest at sunset, and so on);
  • reject the shadow explanation: the Earth’s shadow makes eclipses, not phases.

Before class

  • MoonPhases.key (17 slides, a drawing day; there is a PDF export in Slides/). Blank paper for every student.
  • A lamp at the front and a ball on a stick (or a styrofoam ball per pair) for the classic demonstration, if the room can be darkened.
  • Check tonight’s Moon in the planetarium: if it is up in the evening, end class with “go and look”.

Kickoff

On last class (powers of ten and ratios): one quick scaling question. Keep it light; today is about pictures, not numbers.

The 50 minutes

0–8Kickoff. While it runs, ask the room: “Why does the Moon have phases?” Collect answers without judging; the shadow answer will come up. Write it on the board to come back to.
8–18The demonstration. Lamp = Sun, a student’s head = Earth, ball at arm’s length = Moon. Turn slowly counterclockwise (seen from above) and watch the lit part grow, then shrink. Point out that the lit half always faces the lamp. Then: was the student’s head ever in the way? (Only at full, and only if the ball passes exactly behind it: that is an eclipse.)
18–25The drawing method (MoonPhases.key): draw the Earth from above the North Pole with a person on it standing at the time of day; then the Sun; then the Moon where the phase says. Rotation is counterclockwise, so the sky turns east to west. The “b/d” mnemonic for waxing/waning quarters.
25–42Drawing exercises, in pairs, from the deck: new Moon at sunrise and at noon; full Moon at midnight; third-quarter Moon at sunrise; “what time is it, and where is the Moon?”; “what will this look like at sunrise?”. Walk the room; have two pairs draw on the board. Think-pair-share on the hardest one.
42–48Back to the board: the shadow answer. Ask the class to use their drawings to show why it cannot be right (the Earth’s shadow always points straight away from the Sun, so at first quarter it points off at right angles to the Moon).
48–50Tonight: where and what phase is the Moon? (Check in the planetarium first, then outside.)

Watch for

  • The shadow model (the most common one): handled above, with the students’ own drawings.
  • “The Moon is only out at night”: the first-quarter Moon rises at noon, the last quarter is up all morning. The drawing method makes this visible.
  • Mixing up waxing and waning, and which side is lit: from the Northern Hemisphere a waxing Moon is lit on the right (the Sun side, toward the west in the evening).
  • Drawing the Moon’s orbit tiny around the Earth and the Sun right beside it: say that the Sun is effectively infinitely far, so its rays are parallel across the picture.
