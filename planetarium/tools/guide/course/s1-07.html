By the end of class, students can

  • measure the two kinds of month in the planetarium and explain why the synodic month (phase to phase) is about two days longer than the sidereal month (star to star);
  • measure the lunar day, and say why the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day;
  • time a moonrise and turn the time into the Moon’s angular size: their first real measurement of the sky;
  • (groups that finish) see the Moon’s size change between perigee and apogee.

Before class

  • Students bring laptops (charged); a few spares for those without. Open each lab once on the classroom network to warm the cache.
  • Three labs in one period is too much for one group, so run it as a jigsaw (as in the May 2026 group homework): half the room does Two kinds of month, half does Timing a moonrise; pairs within each half. The Moon’s oval orbit is the extension for whoever finishes.
  • Students should have skimmed the guide for their lab (assigned at the end of last class).

Kickoff

On last class: reading a phase and a time of day off a geocentric drawing.

The 50 minutes

0–7Kickoff. Then the question that ties both labs together: “The Moon goes round us once a month. So why does it rise later every day?” Take a guess or two; don’t answer.
7–12Set up. Show how to open a lab (Labs button or the guide’s link), the lab card’s steps, the stopwatch and marking times (X), stepping the clock a second at a time (Shift + →). Assign halves.
12–38Labs in pairs. Circulate. Months group: they need the Events finder for full Moons and the date box for star-to-star; push them to average several months. Moonrise group: the two marks (top edge on the horizon, bottom edge clear), then the lunar day from two meridian crossings. Anyone done early: the oval-orbit extension.
38–47Jigsaw report-back. Pair a months pair with a moonrise pair: each explains its result to the other in two minutes. Then the whole room: the months group’s two numbers on the board, the moonrise group’s angular sizes (expect a spread: that is real measurement). Answer the opening question together: the Moon moves about 13° a day along its orbit, so the Earth must turn that much extra, about 50 minutes.
47–50What goes in the write-up (the lab card’s steps, their numbers, one sentence on the opening question) and when it is due.

Watch for

  • Moonrise pairs leaving refraction on: the lab switches it off; if they turned it on, their rise time comes out long. A good thing to discuss rather than prevent.
  • Using 24 hours for the lunar day: the lab has them measure it; check that they did.
  • Months pairs using a single month: one month can be hours off the average.
  • Pairs where one student does all the clicking: have them swap at the second measurement.

Lab guides

  • Two kinds of month, and the lunar day
  • How big does the Moon look? Timing a moonrise
  • The Moon’s oval orbit (extension)

After class

  • Write-up due before the next activity. Grade mostly on completing the steps; a result within a wide tolerance is fine.
  • Reading for next class: OpenStax §4.2 (seasons).
