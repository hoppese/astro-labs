Galaxy Rotation & Dark Matter

What this shows

Every star and gas cloud in a spiral galaxy orbits the centre, and its speed is set by how much mass lies inside its orbit. Most of the light comes from the inner few kiloparsecs, so if the light were all the mass, speeds would fall off beyond it (like the planets around the Sun). Instead, measured speeds stay nearly flat (about 220–230 km/s for the Milky Way) far beyond the visible disc. The extra, unseen mass is the dark matter halo; it outweighs the stars and gas several times over.

How to use

Press Play. The four bright tracer stars start in a line; watch how fast each goes round. Pick No dark matter and compare the dashed “visible only” curve with the observed points. Slide the halo mass until the model fits the points. Click the galaxy or drag on the graph to choose a radius; the panel gives the speed there and the mass inside it, M = v²r/G. Solar system shows what a true Keplerian (all-mass-in-the-middle) curve looks like.

The bright spiral arms are drawn as a density wave: a pattern that turns slowly and rigidly (25 km/s/kpc here) while the stars, gas and tracers orbit at their own speeds and drift through it. If the arms were made of the same stars for good, the faster inner orbits would wind them up within a few hundred million years (the “winding problem”).

Worked example — weighing the Milky Way with the Sun

The Sun orbits the centre of the Galaxy at r = 8.0 kpc with speed v = 220 km/s. (a) How long is one orbit? (b) How much mass lies inside the Sun’s orbit? (c) Stars at 16 kpc move at about the same speed. How much mass lies inside 16 kpc, and how does that compare with the roughly 7 × 1010 M☉ of stars and gas we can see?

Twice the mass, but almost no extra light: stars and gas are only about 7 × 1010 M☉, so about 60% of the mass inside 16 kpc is dark. (Check: put r = 16 kpc in the sim.)
