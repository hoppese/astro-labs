Moon Phases

What this shows

The Sun always lights exactly one half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth (about once every 29.5 days), we see that lit half from a changing angle — sometimes face-on (full), sometimes edge-on (quarter), sometimes from behind (new). The phase is set entirely by the Sun–Moon–Earth angle. Earth’s shadow has nothing to do with it (that’s a lunar eclipse, which is rare).

How to use

Drag the Moon around its orbit (or use the Days since new Moon slider, or Play month) and watch the space view: the Moon’s lit half always faces the Sun, while the dashed ring marks the half that faces Earth. The phase is how much of the lit half falls inside that ring. The lower panel is the same Moon seen from Earth. Play speed sets how many days of the month pass each second. Switch hemispheres to see the whole face turn upside down, and switch the space view between tilted and straight down.

Relevant relationships
illuminated fraction from the elongation angle θ (Sun–Earth–Moon). θ = 0° new, 180° full.
days since new Moon, from θ. One synodic month is 29.53 d. (Astronomers call this number the “age of the Moon”; it starts over at every new Moon and has nothing to do with how old the Moon is.)
rough moonrise: the Moon rises about 49 min later each day (24 h ÷ 29.5), ~6 AM at new, noon at first quarter, 6 PM at full.
About the Moon picture

The lower panel is the real near side: colour and elevation maps from NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, lit from the Sun’s true direction, with the relief exaggerated a little. The same face points at Earth all month. The Moon’s slight monthly wobble (libration) and its changing apparent size are not shown. The faint glow on the dark part is earthshine, sunlight reflected off Earth; it is strongest near new Moon, when the Moon’s sky has a nearly full Earth in it, and is drawn brighter here than it looks to the eye. A real new Moon is also lost in the Sun’s glare.
Imagery: NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio, CGI Moon Kit (Visualizer: Ernie Wright; Scientist: Noah Petro). Public domain.

Why not an eclipse every month?

The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth–Sun plane, so at most new and full moons the Moon passes above or below the exact line-up. Eclipses only happen on the few occasions each year when a new or full moon lands near that crossing point.

Worked example — 7 days after new Moon

Tonight the Moon is 7 days past new. What phase is it, roughly how lit is it, and when is it up? Assume you are in the Northern hemisphere.

1 · Turn days since new Moon into an angle
7 days is a bit under a quarter of the 29.53-day cycle, so θ ≈ 85° (just before first quarter).
2 · Illuminated fraction
About 46% lit and growing → waxing crescent, nearly first quarter. In the N. hemisphere the lit edge is on the right.
3 · When is it visible?
First quarter rises around noon and sets around midnight, so the Moon 7 days after new is highest around sunset and sets a little before midnight: the classic “there’s the Moon in the afternoon sky” phase.
