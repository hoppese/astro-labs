What this shows

The Sun always lights exactly one half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth (about once every 29.5 days), we see that lit half from a changing angle — sometimes face-on (full), sometimes edge-on (quarter), sometimes from behind (new). The phase is set entirely by the Sun–Moon–Earth angle. Earth’s shadow has nothing to do with it (that’s a lunar eclipse, which is rare).

How to use

Drag the age slider or hit Play month and watch the top view: the Moon’s lit side always points at the Sun. The lower panel is the same Moon seen from Earth. Use the quarter buttons to jump to the named phases, and switch hemispheres to see why the lit edge flips.

Relevant relationships
illuminated fraction from the elongation angle θ (Sun–Earth–Moon). θ = 0° new, 180° full.
the Moon’s age in days from θ — one synodic month is 29.53 d.
rough moonrise: the Moon rises about 50 min later each day, ~6 AM at new, noon at first quarter, 6 PM at full.
Why not an eclipse every month?

The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth–Sun plane, so at most new and full moons the Moon passes above or below the exact line-up. Eclipses only happen on the few occasions each year when a new or full moon lands near that crossing point.

Worked example — a 7-day-old Moon ×

Tonight the Moon is 7 days past new. What phase is it, roughly how lit is it, and when is it up? Assume you are in the Northern hemisphere.

1 · Turn age into an angle
7 days is a bit under a quarter of the 29.53-day cycle, so θ ≈ 85° — just before first quarter.
2 · Illuminated fraction
About 46% lit and growing → waxing crescent, nearly first quarter. In the N. hemisphere the lit edge is on the right.
3 · When is it visible?
First quarter rises around noon and sets around midnight, so a 7-day Moon is highest in the early evening and sets a little before midnight — the classic “there’s the Moon in the afternoon sky” phase.
