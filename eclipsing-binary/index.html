Eclipsing Binary ×

What this shows

Roughly half of all stars come in pairs. If a binary’s orbit is tipped nearly edge-on to us, each star passes in front of the other once per orbit and the combined light dips. The deeper dip happens when the cooler star blocks the hotter one (more surface brightness hidden); the shallower dip is the reverse. From the shape and timing of those dips alone — no image needed — astronomers pull out the stars’ relative sizes, temperatures, and (with velocities) their masses. Eclipsing binaries are the main source of directly measured stellar masses.

How to use

Watch the light curve build as the stars orbit. Make the stars different sizes and temperatures and see how the two dips change depth. Tilt the orbit (lower the inclination) and the eclipses get shallower, then vanish once the tilt is too big for either disc to cross the other from our line of sight.

Relevant relationships
a star’s contribution to the total light — area times surface brightness (∝ T⁴).
eclipses occur only if the orbit is tilted by less than this from edge-on.
the ratio of the two dip depths gives the ratio of the stars’ surface brightnesses.

Worked example — reading a light curve ×

An eclipsing binary shows a deep eclipse that removes 60% of the light and a shallow one that removes 20%. The two eclipses are equally long. What does that tell you?

1 · Equal-length eclipses → both discs fully overlap
Each eclipse lasts the same time, so the smaller star passes completely across (and behind) the larger one. The flat bottoms confirm a total eclipse.
2 · Depth ratio → surface-brightness ratio
3 · Interpret
The hotter star’s surface is 3× brighter per unit area, so its temperature is about 31/4 ≈ 1.3× higher. The deep eclipse is the one where the cool star covers the hot one.
