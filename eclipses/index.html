Eclipses

What this shows

An eclipse is just one body’s shadow falling on another. A solar eclipse needs a new Moon whose shadow reaches Earth; a lunar eclipse needs a full Moon that passes into Earth’s shadow. Both require the Sun, Earth and Moon to be very nearly in a straight line — and because the Moon’s orbit is tilted 5°, that only happens when a new or full Moon lands near a node (where the tilted orbit crosses the Earth–Sun plane). That’s why we get eclipses in short “seasons” about twice a year, not every month.

How to use

The slider sets how far past a node the Moon is when it lines up with the Sun (new) or opposite it (full). In the tilted orbit view (drag to turn it) watch the Moon rise above or sink below the Earth–Sun plane and its shadow miss Earth; the side view shows the umbra and penumbra with heights to scale. The lower panel is what you would see. Change the Moon’s distance to flip a central solar eclipse between total (Moon looks slightly bigger than the Sun) and annular (Moon looks smaller, leaving a ring).

Worked example — is this new Moon a solar eclipse?

A new Moon occurs when the Moon is 3° (in orbit) from its ascending node. Does the Sun get eclipsed anywhere on Earth?

1 · Moon’s latitude at that moment
2 · Compare with the eclipse limit
The Sun and Moon each have an angular radius of about 0.26°. Observers spread across Earth see the Moon shifted by up to 0.95° (Earth’s radius as seen from the Moon), so a partial solar eclipse happens somewhere only if the Moon’s latitude is under about 0.26° + 0.26° + 0.95° ≈ 1.5°.
3 · Verdict
0.27° < 1.5°, so yes. In fact it is well inside the central limit (about 0.95°), so somewhere on Earth sees a total or annular eclipse; everywhere nearby sees a partial one.
