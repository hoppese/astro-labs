What this shows

An eclipse is just one body’s shadow falling on another. A solar eclipse needs a new Moon whose shadow reaches Earth; a lunar eclipse needs a full Moon that passes into Earth’s shadow. Both require the Sun, Earth and Moon to be very nearly in a straight line — and because the Moon’s orbit is tilted 5°, that only happens when a new or full Moon lands near a node (where the tilted orbit crosses the Earth–Sun plane). That’s why we get eclipses in short “seasons” about twice a year, not every month.

How to use

Slide the Moon toward the node and watch the shadow line up. The lower panel is what you’d see: the Sun with a bite taken out of it (solar) or the Moon sliding into Earth’s reddish shadow (lunar). Change the Moon’s distance to flip a central solar eclipse between total (Moon looks slightly bigger than the Sun) and annular (Moon looks smaller, leaving a ring).

3 · Verdict
0.27° < 1.5° — yes. In fact it is well inside the central limit (~0.9°), so somewhere on Earth sees a total or annular eclipse; everywhere nearby sees a partial one.
