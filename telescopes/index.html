Telescopes ×

What this shows

A telescope’s most important number is its aperture D, the diameter of the main lens or mirror. Aperture buys two things. Light-gathering power grows with the collecting area, ∝ D²: double the diameter and you collect 4× the light, so you see fainter objects. Resolving power improves as 1/D: light waves spread (diffract) as they pass through the opening, so every star is imaged as a small disk with rings, of angular size about 1.22λ/D. Two stars closer than that blur together.

Magnification is set by the eyepiece, M = fobj/feye, and is cheap: swap eyepieces. It enlarges the blur along with the stars, so past a point it shows no new detail (“empty magnification”). On the ground, the turbulent atmosphere (seeing) blurs stars to about 1″, which is why big telescopes use adaptive optics or go to space.

How to use

Pick a telescope or drag the aperture. The ray diagram shows how the objective (lens or mirror) brings starlight to a focus and the eyepiece turns it into a magnified view; the off-axis star (green) shows angles being multiplied by M.

The left panel shows the same patch of sky with the stars bright enough to detect. The right panel is a double star: watch it go from one blob to two as D grows or λ shrinks, then crank the magnification on an unresolved pair and see that you only get a bigger blob. Turn on seeing to add the atmosphere. The profile under the image is the brightness along the line through both stars.

Worked example: a backyard telescope vs your eye ×

A 200 mm (8-inch) reflector has fobj = 2000 mm and comes with a 25 mm eyepiece. Compare its light-gathering with your 7 mm pupil, find its resolution in green light (550 nm), and its magnification. Can it split a double star 1.0″ apart?

1 · Light-gathering power
It collects about 800 times as much light as your eye, so it shows stars about 7 magnitudes fainter (to roughly magnitude 13).
2 · Resolution
A 1.0″ pair is wider than 0.69″, so the telescope resolves it, at least above the atmosphere. On a typical night seeing blurs each star to about 1″, which smears this pair into one (set seeing to 1.0″ and see).
3 · Magnification
At 80× the pair appears 80″ apart, a bit more than the eye’s limit of about 1′ (60″), so you can just see two stars. A 10 mm eyepiece (200×) makes them easier to see, but cannot split a pair closer than 0.69″: it only enlarges the blur.
