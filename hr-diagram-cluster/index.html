Cluster Ages

What this shows

Every star in a cluster formed from the same cloud at essentially the same time, so a cluster is a set of stars of one age but a whole range of masses. The most massive stars burn out fastest, so as the cluster ages the top of the main sequence “peels away” — stars there swell into red giants and then fade to white dwarfs. The point where stars are just now leaving the main sequence is the turnoff, and the main-sequence lifetime of a star at the turnoff equals the cluster’s age. Fit the turnoff, get the age.

How to use

Drag the age slider (or hit Age the cluster) and watch the turnoff point slide down the main sequence while red giants pile up in the upper right. Load a real cluster to see its turnoff: the Pleiades still has hot blue B stars on its main sequence, so it’s young; M13’s turnoff is down at a Sun-like mass, so it’s ~12 billion years old. (Every star here has the Sun’s composition; real globular clusters are poor in heavy elements, so their turnoff stars are a little hotter and bluer than shown.)

Relevant relationships
main-sequence lifetime: fuel (∝ M) over burn rate (∝ L); steeply shorter for massive stars (M in solar masses).
rule-of-thumb turnoff mass: the star whose lifetime equals the cluster age t.
how luminous the turnoff star is: the sim reads it (and its temperature) from a table of real main-sequence stars, so its ages can differ from the rule of thumb by ~30%.

Worked example — the age of M67

The open cluster M67 has a main-sequence turnoff at about 1.3 solar masses. How old is it?

1 · Rule of thumb: the turnoff star’s lifetime = the cluster’s age
2 · Better: use the star’s actual luminosity (a 1.3 M☉ star has L ≈ 2.8 L☉)
3 · Interpret
About 4 to 5 billion years, close to the Sun’s own age; detailed stellar models give about 4 Gyr. That makes M67 one of the older open clusters: most open clusters break apart long before they get this old.
