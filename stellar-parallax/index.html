Stellar Parallax

What this shows

Hold up a finger and blink between your eyes: it jumps against the background. Do the same with a nearby star using the two ends of Earth’s orbit (six months apart, 2 AU apart) as your two eyes, and the star traces a tiny ellipse against the far more distant background stars over a year. Half the full swing is the parallax angle p: the angle 1 AU subtends as seen from the star. The distance in parsecs is simply 1/p with p in arcseconds; that is what “parsec” (parallax second) means. This is the one direct rung on the whole cosmic distance ladder.

How to use

Pick a star or set a distance, then run the year. The 3D view (not to scale) shows the sight line from Earth, through the nearby star, landing on the far background; as Earth goes around the Sun that landing point traces an ellipse. The lower panel shows the same ellipse on the sky at its true angular size, with a scale bar. Move the star farther away and the ellipse shrinks toward a dot; tick magnify to blow it back up. The angle β sets the ellipse’s shape: a star above the pole of Earth’s orbit traces a circle, one in the plane of the orbit just slides back and forth along a line.

Relevant relationships
distance in parsecs = 1 over the parallax angle in arcseconds.
the small-angle geometry it comes from: baseline 1 AU, angle p.
1 parsec in other units: about 3.26 light-years, or 206,265 AU.

Worked example — the distance to 61 Cygni

In 1838 Bessel measured a parallax of 0.314 arcseconds for 61 Cygni, the first stellar distance ever found. How far away is it?

1 · The parsec relation
2 · In light-years
3 · Interpret
About 10.4 light-years. The modern parallax is 0.286″, which gives 3.50 pc (11.4 ly): Bessel was within 10%. An angle of 0.3″ is the width of a coin seen from about 14 km, which is why no one managed it before precise telescopes. Try the 61 Cygni button.
