Stellar Parallax

What this shows

Hold up a finger and blink between your eyes: it jumps against the background. Do the same with a nearby star using the two ends of Earth’s orbit (six months apart, 2 AU apart) as your two eyes, and the star appears to trace a tiny ellipse against the far more distant background stars over a year (the star itself has not moved; your viewpoint has). Half the full swing is the parallax angle p: the angle 1 AU subtends as seen from the star. The distance in parsecs is simply 1/p with p in arcseconds; that is what “parsec” (parallax second) means. This is the one direct rung on the whole cosmic distance ladder.

How to use

Pick a star or set a distance, then run the year. In space (top, not to scale) the star never moves: Earth goes round the Sun, so the sight line from Earth through the star pivots about the star and its far end sweeps over the far-away background stars. The two dashed sight lines, six months apart, are the extremes; the angle between them at the star is 2p, and the triangle Sun, Earth, star (right angle at the Sun, base 1 AU) defines p. From Earth (bottom; north up, east left, as on the sky) the same thing is seen as the star shifting against the fixed background stars, always toward the side of the sky the Sun is on. With auto-zoom the view magnifies in steps as the path shrinks (watch the scale bar); untick it to see how small the shift of a distant star really is. The angle β is the star’s ecliptic latitude (negative = south of Earth’s orbit plane) and sets the path’s shape: a circle at the pole of the orbit (β = ±90°), a back-and-forth line in its plane (β = 0), and an ellipse p by p sin β in between. The dates are real for the named stars. For a star north of the orbit plane the space view shows the background screen from its far side, so east and west are mirrored compared with the view from Earth: follow the E and N arrows.

Relevant relationships
distance in parsecs = 1 over the parallax angle in arcseconds.
the small-angle geometry it comes from: baseline 1 AU, angle p.
1 parsec in other units: about 3.26 light-years, or 206,265 AU.

Worked example — the distance to 61 Cygni

In 1838 Bessel measured a parallax of 0.314 arcseconds for 61 Cygni, the first stellar distance ever found. How far away is it?

1 · The parsec relation
2 · In light-years
3 · Interpret
About 10.4 light-years. The modern parallax is 0.286″, which gives 3.50 pc (11.4 ly): Bessel was within 10%. An angle of 0.3″ is the width of a coin seen from about 14 km, which is why no one managed it before precise telescopes. Try the 61 Cygni button.
