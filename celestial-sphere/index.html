The Celestial Sphere ×

What this shows

Pretend the stars are painted on a giant sphere that turns once a day around the line through Earth’s poles. From where you stand you see half of it — the dome above your horizon. The celestial pole sits at an altitude equal to your latitude, and everything wheels around it. Stars close enough to the pole (declination > 90° − latitude) never dip below the horizon — they are circumpolar. Stars far on the other side never come up at all.

How to use

Set your latitude, pick a star (or dial its declination), and drag the time slider or hit Run the night. The map shows the star’s whole daily circle; the solid arc is above the horizon, the dashed part is below. The lower strip is its altitude over the night — the shaded band is when it is up.

Relevant relationships
altitude of a star from your latitude φ, its declination δ, and the hour angle H (0 at transit, 15° per hour).

Worked example — is the Big Dipper circumpolar from Chicago? ×

Chicago is at latitude 42° N. The lowest star of the Big Dipper’s bowl has declination about +54°. Does it ever set?

1 · Circumpolar limit
Any star with δ > 48° stays above the horizon all night.
2 · Compare
+54° > 48° — yes, it is circumpolar. The whole Dipper wheels around Polaris without ever setting.
3 · From Miami (26° N)?
Limit is 90° − 26° = 64°. Now +54° < 64°, so from Miami the Dipper dips below the northern horizon for part of the night.
