Exoplanet Transit

What this shows

If a planet’s orbit is lined up edge-on to us, once per orbit it crosses in front of its star and blocks a sliver of light. The star dims by the ratio of the two discs’ areas — typically a fraction of a percent — then recovers. Measure the depth and you get the planet’s size; measure the spacing of the dips and you get its orbital period; the shape of the ingress and egress tells you how centrally it crossed. This is how most known exoplanets were found.

How to use

Set a planet size and orbit and hit Play. On the left, a side view shows how far the orbit is tilted from edge-on; the planet transits only if it passes through the gold band in front of the star. On the right is what a telescope would see if it could resolve the star, and the lower panel builds the light curve. Grow the planet and the dip deepens as the square of its radius. Slide the impact parameter b toward 1 and the crossing gets shorter and more V-shaped; past 1 + Rp/R★ there is no transit at all. Turn on limb darkening to see why real transits have rounded bottoms.

Relevant relationships
transit depth = the disc-area ratio (uniform star).
period from Kepler’s third law (star mass in solar units).
transit duration (k = Rp/R★): longer for a big star, a close orbit, and a central crossing.
impact parameter: how far from the star’s centre the planet crosses, in stellar radii, set by the inclination i.

Worked example — how deep is a Jupiter transit?

A Jupiter-sized planet (R = 11.2 R⊕) transits a Sun-sized star. How much does the star dim, and what about an Earth-sized planet?
