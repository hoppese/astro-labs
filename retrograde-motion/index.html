Retrograde Motion

What this shows

Most of the time a planet drifts slowly eastward against the background stars. But whenever Earth and the planet pass each other, the planet appears to stop, back up westward, stop again and carry on. Nothing about its orbit changes: it is a line-of-sight effect of the faster, inner planet overtaking the slower, outer one.

Mars, Jupiter, Saturn: Earth is the faster one. They reverse around opposition, high in the midnight sky.

Mercury, Venus: they overtake Earth. They reverse around inferior conjunction, nearly in line with the Sun, so most of the reversal is lost in its glare. They never reach opposition: they swing from greatest elongation east of the Sun (evening sky) to greatest elongation west of it (morning sky) and back.

How to use

Hit Play. The top panel shows the orbits from above (all planets go counter-clockwise); the sight line runs from Earth through the planet to the distant stars. The bottom panel is where the planet appears among the stars, with east on the left as you face south from the Northern hemisphere.

The bottom window stays still through every reversal, so what you see there is the motion against the stars. Between reversals it slides along to keep up; the stars and the longitude scale slide with it. Numbered dots mark the same instants in both panels.

Speed 1× is 64 days per second (16 for Mercury, whose whole cycle is only 116 days).

Relevant relationships
each planet’s angular position, from Kepler’s third law P² ∝ a³.
the planet’s direction as seen from Earth — retrograde whenever this angle is decreasing.
synodic period: the time from one opposition (or inferior conjunction) to the next, i.e. between retrograde loops.
The model, and how good it is

Circular orbits at constant speed, with each orbit’s real period, size, tilt and node. Starting positions are chosen for convenience and are not dates; the stars are invented. Real orbits are ellipses, so real reversals vary from one to the next. Mercury (eccentricity 0.21) is the worst case: its real greatest elongation runs from 18° to 28°, where this model always gives about 23°.
