Retrograde Motion

What this shows

Most of the time an outer planet drifts slowly eastward against the background stars. But around opposition — when Earth, on its faster inside track, catches up and passes it — the planet appears to stop, back up westward for a few weeks, then resume. Nothing about the planet changes; it is entirely a line-of-sight effect of Earth overtaking it. Explaining this without epicycles was one of the first big wins for the Sun-centred model.

How to use

Hit Play. The top panel is the real layout of the orbits seen from above (both planets go counter-clockwise); the sight line runs from Earth through the planet out to the distant stars. The bottom panel is where the planet appears among the stars, drawn as you would see it facing south from the Northern hemisphere, so east is on the left. The numbered dots are the same instants in both panels: watch the sight line swing backward while Earth overtakes the planet. Switch planets to see how a slower, more distant planet makes a smaller loop, more often.

Relevant relationships
each planet’s angular position, from Kepler’s third law P² ∝ a³.
the planet’s direction as seen from Earth — retrograde whenever this angle is decreasing.

Earth laps Mars every 2.14 years, so Mars goes retrograde a little less often than every two years, for about 10 weeks each time. Jupiter, much slower, gets lapped every 1.09 years: almost annually.
