What this shows

Earth’s axis is tilted 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way all year. So for half the year your hemisphere leans toward the Sun and for half it leans away. Leaning toward the Sun does two things: the noon Sun climbs higher (its light hits the ground more squarely, concentrating the energy) and the days get longer. Both add heat. That is summer. Distance from the Sun barely matters — Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.

How to use

Drag the date through the year and watch the top view: the axis never changes direction, but which hemisphere catches the Sun does. Pick your latitude and read off the noon Sun altitude and the length of the day. The lower panel shows how the same sunbeam spreads over more ground when the Sun is low.

Relevant relationships
solar declination δ — the latitude where the Sun is straight overhead at noon. Swings between ±23.5° over the year.

How high is the noon Sun at latitude 40° N on the June solstice, and how does that compare with the December solstice?

1 · Declination at each solstice
June: δ = +23.5°. December: δ = −23.5°.
2 · Noon Sun altitude, a = 90° − |φ − δ|
3 · The difference
73.5° vs 26.5° — a 47° swing, exactly twice the tilt. In June the sunlight lands nearly straight down and the day is ~15 h long; in December it comes in at a low slant and the day is ~9 h. That is the whole reason for the seasons.
