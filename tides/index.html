What this shows

The Moon pulls hardest on the ocean facing it, less hard on the solid Earth, and least of all on the ocean on the far side. Subtract the pull on Earth’s centre and what’s left — the tidal force — stretches the oceans into two bulges, one toward the Moon and one directly away. Earth rotates through both, so most coasts get two high tides a day. The Sun raises its own smaller pair of bulges; when Sun and Moon line up (new or full Moon) the bulges add for big spring tides, and when they’re at right angles (quarter Moon) they partly cancel for weak neap tides.

How to use

Move the Moon around its orbit and watch the bulge swing to follow it — and the tide range in the lower panel grow and shrink. Spin the Earth (or drag the time slider) to sweep your location through the bulges and trace the two-high-tides-a-day curve. Toggle the Sun off to see how much of the effect is lunar.

Relevant relationships
the tidal force scales with mass over distance cubed — not squared — which is why the closer, lighter Moon beats the Sun.
the height pattern around Earth: high at 0° and 180° from the body, low at 90°.
