Phases of Venus & Planetary Configurations

What this shows

Venus and Mercury orbit inside Earth’s orbit, so from Earth they never stray far from the Sun: at most about 46–47° for Venus and 18–28° for Mercury (its orbit is quite elliptical). That farthest angle is the greatest elongation. East of the Sun the planet is an evening star; west of it, a morning star. Passing between Earth and the Sun is inferior conjunction; passing behind the Sun, superior conjunction.

Because the planet’s distance from us changes so much, its phase and apparent size change together: a big thin crescent near inferior conjunction, a small nearly full disc near superior conjunction.

How to use

Press Play or drag the date. Watch the elongation angle at Earth, the telescope view (phase and size, drawn to one scale for both planets), and where the planet sits in the sky beside the Sun. The strip of nine images is one whole cycle.

Switch to Ptolemy. Venus now rides an epicycle whose centre stays on the Earth–Sun line, always closer to us than the Sun. It reaches the same places in the sky, but its lit side always faces mostly away from us: it can never look gibbous or full. Try the epicycle-centre slider to make it gibbous; you can’t without putting Venus beyond the Sun. When Galileo saw a small, nearly full Venus in 1610, Ptolemy’s arrangement was ruled out.

Fair caveat: Tycho Brahe’s hybrid (planets circle the Sun, the Sun circles Earth) gives exactly the Copernican phases, so the phases alone did not decide between Tycho and Copernicus.

Relevant relationships
greatest elongation for circular orbits (a in AU): the sight line is tangent to the orbit.
synodic period S (one full cycle of phases as seen from Earth) from the sidereal periods.
illuminated fraction from the phase angle α (Sun–planet–Earth): α < 90° is gibbous, α > 90° crescent.
angular diameter of a planet of radius R at distance d (small-angle formula).

Worked example — sizing up Venus

At greatest elongation Venus is 46.3° from the Sun. (a) How far is Venus from the Sun? (b) How big does Venus look (radius 6052 km) at inferior and at superior conjunction? (c) Venus takes 224.7 days to orbit the Sun. How often does it return to the same phase as seen from Earth?

1 · (a) At greatest elongation the Earth–Venus line is tangent to Venus’s orbit
2 · (b) Distances at the two conjunctions
3 · (b) Angular diameters (1 AU = 1.496 × 108 km, 1 rad = 206 265″)
Venus looks about 6× wider as a thin crescent than as a nearly full disc.
4 · (c) Synodic period
Venus laps Earth once every 1.6 years, so each evening-star or morning-star season comes round about every 19 months. (Check: inferior conjunctions on 2026 Oct 24 and 2028 Jun 1 in the sim.)
