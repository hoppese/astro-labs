H–R Diagram

What this shows

Plot stars by surface temperature (hot on the left, by tradition) and luminosity, and they don’t scatter randomly — they fall into a few groups. About 90% sit on the main sequence, the diagonal strip where a star spends its long hydrogen-burning life. Cool but bright stars in the upper right must be huge: they are giants and supergiants. Hot but faint stars in the lower left are tiny, dense white dwarfs — dead stellar cores. Because L = 4πR²σT⁴, position on this diagram tells you a star’s radius.

How to use

Drop one of the named stars, drag the sliders, or click anywhere on the diagram. The dashed diagonals are lines of constant radius (labelled in solar radii, R☉). Watch where your star lands: on the main sequence you also get a rough mass and lifetime; off it, the label tells you what kind of object has that temperature and luminosity. White dwarfs get their own spectral types: DA plus a number equal to 50,400 K / T (Sirius B is DA2). Random problem drops a realistic star from one of the groups.

Relevant relationships
luminosity from radius and temperature — the constant-R diagonals.
main-sequence mass–luminosity relation (rough).
main-sequence lifetime — massive stars burn out fast.

Worked example — how big is Betelgeuse?

Betelgeuse has a surface temperature of ~3600 K and a luminosity of about 90,000 L⊙. What is its radius?

About 770 solar radii (3.6 AU): if it replaced the Sun, its surface would reach past the orbit of Mars and most of the way to Jupiter. That is what puts it in the supergiant corner of the diagram.
