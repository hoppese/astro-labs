What this shows

A star’s spectrum pins down two things: its surface temperature (the spectral type — the OBAFGKM sequence, hot to cool) and its size / luminosity (the luminosity class, I = supergiant down to V = main-sequence dwarf, plus WD for white dwarfs). Together they place the star in a specific band of the H–R diagram, and from that band you can read off its absolute magnitude — which, compared with how bright it looks, gives its distance (“spectroscopic parallax”).

How to use

Pick a luminosity class, then slide the spectral type from O to M. The crosshair tracks the band on the diagram; the panel gives the full type (e.g. G2 V for the Sun) and the range of absolute magnitudes stars of that type and class have. Compare a K0 III giant with a K0 V dwarf — same temperature, wildly different luminosity.

The classification
Spectral type — O B A F G K M, each split 0–9, running from ~40,000 K (O) to ~3,000 K (M). “Oh Be A Fine Guy/Girl, Kiss Me.”
Luminosity class — Ia/Ib bright/normal supergiant, II bright giant, III giant, IV subgiant, V main sequence (dwarf).
The Sun is a G2 V star: 5,772 K, on the main sequence, absolute magnitude +4.8.
