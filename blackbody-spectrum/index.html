What this shows

Any dense, hot object glows with a smooth spectrum whose shape depends on only one thing: its temperature. Hotter means brighter at every wavelength and a peak that slides toward the blue. That is why a cool star looks red, the Sun looks yellow-white, and a hot star looks blue — and why an iron bar goes from dull red to orange to white as it heats. Split a star’s light and the peak of its continuum tells you its surface temperature.

How to use

Drag the temperature and watch the curve. The shaded band is the visible range; the dashed line marks the peak (Wien’s law). Keep the Sun’s curve on for comparison — a small temperature change makes a huge difference in total output, because it goes as T⁴. Normalise the peak if you only care about the shape (the colour).

Relevant relationships
the Planck function — brightness per wavelength at temperature T.
Wien’s law: the peak wavelength, in nanometres, from the temperature in kelvin.
Stefan–Boltzmann: total energy radiated per area — fourth power of T.

Worked example — the colour of Betelgeuse ×

Betelgeuse has a surface temperature of about 3500 K. Where does its spectrum peak, and how does its per-area output compare with the Sun’s 5772 K?

1 · Wien’s law
828 nm — in the near infrared, past the red end of what the eye sees, so it looks deep red-orange.
2 · Output per area, ∝ T⁴
3 · Interpret
Each square metre of Betelgeuse radiates only ~14% as much as the Sun. It is luminous overall only because it is enormous — hundreds of times the Sun’s radius.
