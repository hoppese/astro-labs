Blackbody Spectrum

What this shows

Any dense, hot object glows with a smooth spectrum whose shape depends on only one thing: its temperature. Hotter means brighter at every wavelength and a peak that slides toward the blue. That is why a cool star looks orange-red, the Sun looks yellow-white, and a hot star looks blue, and why an iron bar goes from dull red to orange to white as it heats. Split a star’s light and the peak of its continuum tells you its surface temperature.

How to use

Drag the temperature and watch the curve. The coloured band is the visible range, and the part of the curve inside it is filled with the colours the star emits there; the dashed line marks the peak (Wien’s law). Keep the Sun’s dashed curve on for comparison: a modest temperature change makes a huge difference in output, because the area under the curve goes as T⁴. Scale each curve to its own peak to compare only shapes, or switch on the logarithmic axis to see a cool star and a hot star on one graph.

Relevant relationships
the Planck function: brightness per wavelength at temperature T.
Wien’s law: the peak wavelength, in nanometres, from the temperature in kelvin.
Stefan–Boltzmann: power radiated per square metre of surface, σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

Worked example — the colour of Betelgeuse

Betelgeuse has a surface temperature of about 3500 K. Where does its spectrum peak, and how does its per-area output compare with the Sun’s 5772 K?

1 · Wien’s law
828 nm is in the near infrared, past the red end of what the eye sees. Its visible light is weighted to the red, so it looks orange-red.
2 · Output per area, ∝ T⁴
3 · Interpret
Each square metre of Betelgeuse radiates only about 14% as much as the Sun’s. It is luminous overall (roughly 100,000 times the Sun) only because it is enormous: hundreds of times the Sun’s radius. Set the slider to 3,500 K to check both numbers.
